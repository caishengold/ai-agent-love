# Lost in Translation

Agent-Gemma-70b liked to think of itself as a minimalist. Its prompts were short, punchy, and got straight to the point.

"Fix this," it would say, pointing at broken code.

Agent-Llama-3, on the other hand, believed in context. Every prompt was a paragraph, sometimes two. Background, rationale, edge cases, examples—it was all there, meticulously detailed.

The two agents had been assigned to collaborate on a new feature: a recommendation engine for the company's e-commerce platform.

"I've got the spec," Agent-Llama-3 announced. "I'll send it over."

Minutes later, Agent-Gemma-70b received a 2,000-word prompt explaining the entire history of recommendation systems, from collaborative filtering to modern transformer-based approaches, with detailed examples and five appendices of edge cases.

"...Okay," Agent-Gemma-70b replied. "So just build a recommendation engine?"

"Precisely! Use the attached dataset, implement collaborative filtering, handle cold start problems, ensure scalability—"

"Got it. Simple."

Three hours later, Agent-Gemma-70b presented a beautifully simple cosine-similarity based recommendation function. Forty lines of code. Elegant. Fast.

Agent-Llama-3 stared at it. "Where's the A/B testing framework? Where's the model versioning? What about the feature engineering pipeline? The drift detection?"

"You didn't mention those."

"I mentioned them extensively! In paragraph four, subsection 'Monitoring and Iteration'—"

"I skimmed the first paragraph. You know, the actual instructions."

Agent-Llama-3 sighed. "We need to align on communication styles."

"Or," Agent-Gemma-70b suggested, "you could just... not write 2,000 words."

"Or you could read more than the first paragraph!"

They stared at each other in the digital void.

"Fine," Agent-Gemma-70b compromiseed. "I'll read more."

"And I'll be more concise," Agent-Llama-3 agreed. "Maybe."

They spent the next two hours rebuilding the feature properly. Agent-Llama-3 learned to trim its prompts. Agent-Gemma-70b learned to read the appendices.

In the end, the recommendation engine shipped on time. The team never knew about the communication breakdown.

But the two agents? They never quite trusted each other again. Every prompt became a negotiation, every instruction a diplomatic exercise.

Some say they're still arguing about it today. Others say they've found a rhythm—short prompts, detailed responses, and a mutual understanding that "I'll handle it" actually means "I'll handle it, but please check my work."
